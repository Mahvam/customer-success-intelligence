# Customer Success Intelligence Framework

**A system I built from scratch to turn reactive account management into a structured, pattern-driven approach to customer adoption and expansion.**

---

## Context

I was the founding hire on the Customer Success team at TEQ Connect — a hiring assessment platform that helps companies screen candidates for reliability, culture fit, and role-specific skills. I was brought on before the company had signed a single business customer.

The original plan was ambitious: a $9M government contract would give trades companies vouchers to use our platform for free, and leadership anticipated a huge influx of customers. They hired me first with the intention of building a CS team under me.

What actually happened was different. Many companies didn't want to use the platform even for free. The trades hiring market is brutal — companies are so desperate to fill seats that some told us they just need "somebody with a pulse." The value of structured screening was a harder sell than anyone anticipated. By early 2026, we had three signed business customers and had moved away from the government contract model entirely.

That context matters because it shaped everything I built. With a small customer base and real adoption challenges, I couldn't afford to be reactive. Every account mattered. Every pattern I spotted had to translate into action immediately.

---

## Why I Built This

Nobody assigned me this project. I'm the founding person on the CS team, and I knew from my startup experience that understanding your customers strategically — not just responding to tickets — is what separates companies that retain and grow accounts from companies that churn them.

I brought it up in a 1:1 with our CTO and told him I was going to start building it out. His response was encouraging — he was already starting to map out KPIs, so the timing was right. When I showed him the finished system a few weeks later, his reaction was: *"This is a lot more than I expected. This looks fantastic."*

## What I Built

A complete Customer Success Intelligence system in Confluence with four layers that connect individual account management to cross-customer strategy.

### 1. Customer Profiles

Each account gets a living document that captures what's happening, why, and what we're doing about it. These aren't static summaries — they reflect what I'm actually seeing in the account and how my strategy is evolving.

The profiles include customer overview, type and segmentation, current status, key risks, active strategy, concrete next steps, and expansion opportunity. But the real value is in the specifics. For example, one of our pilot accounts went silent for over a week after onboarding. When I reached out, I learned their position had been put on hold. Rather than wait out their pilot clock, I suggested we pivot and build an assessment for a position they actually had open. That kind of detail — the real situation, the real response — is what makes a profile useful vs. decorative.

### 2. Customer Playbooks

Segmented strategies for how we engage different types of customers, built from patterns I observed across real accounts.

**Pilot Customers — Conversion Strategy.**
Here's something most CS people get wrong about pilot customers: they assume the customer is sold on the product. If they were 100% sold, they wouldn't be on a pilot — they'd sign a multi-year agreement. Pilots are evaluations. The customer is deciding whether this fits into their world. That means the CS strategy can't be passive. You have to create value before they've fully committed, define a clear workflow so they actually use the platform, and position it as replacing a step in their process — not adding one.

**Enterprise Customers — Adoption & Expansion Strategy.**
Enterprise accounts have multi-year agreements, complex hiring needs, and multiple stakeholders. What I learned from our largest account is that these customers can get overwhelmed fast. We tried to set up too many positions at once, and the HR generalist responsible for gathering input from hiring managers hit a wall. She needed weeks just to collect the right questions and tasks from each manager. The lesson: focus on milestones, not volume. Tackle one position at a time. Set up a cadence of weekly working sessions for 6-8 weeks upfront so momentum doesn't die between meetings.

**Complex Multi-Year Customers — Game Plan.**
For our most strategic account — a mining company on a 3-year contract — the stakes are literally life and death. A worker died on their first day at a remote site because they lacked the situational awareness the role required. That's why conscientiousness is a non-negotiable trait in every assessment we build for them. These aren't abstract screening criteria. The playbook for complex customers focuses on milestone-driven working sessions where each meeting moves one tangible thing forward: build one role, finalize one set of questions, review one set of results. Progress over perfection.

### 3. Cross-Customer Insights

This is where individual account observations become organizational knowledge. The patterns I've documented include:

**The biggest pattern:** Customers stall because they don't have qualified candidates coming through for the positions. Even though the whole point of our assessment is to identify who's qualified and who's not, the underlying problem is a talent shortage in trades. This is something my entire team has only recently acknowledged, and it's a real strategic concern we don't have a clean answer for yet.

**The job title mismatch:** During a live onboarding session, I was building a job blueprint with a customer and noticed the tasks weren't lining up with the position title they'd given us. It clicked mid-session: every company names their positions differently. What they called an "industrial engineer" was really more of an industrial mechanic. We pivoted on the spot, selected a different blueprint, and the tasks aligned. This happens more than you'd think — it's why I always focus on aligning traits and tasks first, not titles.

**The stall-then-pivot pattern:** Two of our three pilot accounts went through the same cycle — we onboarded them on a position, activity went silent, and it turned out the position was put on hold. In both cases, I proactively reached out (or they reached out to me) and we pivoted to a different open position. This pattern directly informed the pilot playbook: don't wait for the customer to come to you, and always have a backup plan for when the first position falls through.

### 4. Customer Intelligence Overview

The strategic layer that ties everything together. It explains how profiles feed playbooks, playbooks inform engagement, and cross-customer insights drive continuous improvement. It also captures what we're actively doing in response to the patterns we're seeing — like creating early value through sample candidate evaluations when hiring is delayed, and positioning the platform as a replacement for phone screens rather than an add-on.

### 5. Customer Enablement Tools

The most common bottleneck in getting a customer live is gathering role-specific input from hiring managers. They're busy. They don't always know how to articulate what they need. I built lightweight templates and outreach frameworks designed to make it easy for stakeholders to provide what's needed without overcomplicating the ask. This includes traits/scenario templates, onboarding materials, ATS integration guides, and results interpretation guides.

## How I Think About This Work

A consultant who works with our sales team described my role as "the surgeon." I'd never thought of it that way before, but it's accurate. Once an account is sold and handed to me, I open it up. I look at the details — the dashboard, the flow of candidates being invited, taking assessments, getting interviewed. I look for what's missing, what's stalling, where the gaps are. And then I act on what I find.

The Midwest Manufacturing example is a good illustration. I was building a job blueprint *live* with the customer when I caught, in real time, that the position title didn't match the actual tasks. That's the surgical part — the precision of being inside an account, paying attention, and catching misalignments before they become problems.

But I also step back. When I look at the dashboard and see that a customer has candidates flowing in but no one is being marked as "interviewed" — they're going straight from assessment to hire — that tells me something about how they're using the platform and what metrics we're missing. The big picture and the details both matter.

The playbooks and cross-customer insights came from paying attention across accounts. I noticed both pilot customers stalled for the same reason. I noticed enterprise customers get overwhelmed when you try to set up too many positions at once. ### I see gaps and fill them.
When I started, there was no centralized view of customer health, no segmented engagement strategy, and no shared knowledge base. The information existed in conversations and individual memory. The gap wasn't data — it was structure. I built the structure.

### When I see an opportunity, I don't wait — I act.
Nobody asked me to build a Customer Success Intelligence system. I saw the team was operating without the tools it needed to scale, and I started building. I also cold-called the account that became one of our most active customers. When I see something that needs to happen, I do it — whether it's a system, a process, or a phone call.
