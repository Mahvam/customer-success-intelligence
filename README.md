# Customer Success Intelligence Framework

A system I built from scratch to turn reactive account management into a structured, pattern driven approach to customer adoption and expansion.

> Note on confidentiality: every customer example in this repository is anonymized and generalized. Names, identifying details, and sensitive specifics have been removed or turned into composites to protect customer and company privacy. The framework, the patterns, and the thinking are real. The specifics are illustrative.

## Context

I was the founding hire on the Customer Success team at an early stage hiring assessment startup, brought on before the company had signed its first business customer. The product sold structured candidate screening into a market still deciding whether it wanted structured screening at all, so from day one I worked with a small customer base and a real adoption challenge.

That context shaped everything I built. With few accounts and a genuinely hard adoption problem, I could not afford to be reactive. Every account mattered. Every pattern I spotted had to turn into action right away.

## Why I Built This

Nobody assigned me this project. As the founding person on the CS team, I knew from earlier startup experience that understanding customers strategically, not just responding to tickets, is what separates companies that retain and grow accounts from companies that churn them.

I raised it with our CTO in a 1:1 and said I was going to build it. He was supportive and already thinking about KPIs, so the timing lined up. When I showed him the finished system a few weeks later, his response was that it went well beyond what he expected.

## What I Built

A complete Customer Success Intelligence system with four connected layers that link individual account management to cross customer strategy.

### 1. Customer Profiles

Each account gets a living document that captures what is happening, why, and what we are doing about it. Not static summaries. They reflect what I am actually seeing and how my strategy is evolving. A profile covers overview, type and segmentation, current status, key risks, active strategy, concrete next steps, and expansion opportunity. The value is in the specifics. Example: one pilot account went quiet for over a week after onboarding. When I reached out, I learned the role they were hiring for had been put on hold. Rather than let the pilot clock run out, I suggested we pivot and build an assessment for a position they actually had open. The real situation and the real response is what makes a profile useful instead of decorative.

### 2. Customer Playbooks

Segmented strategies for how we engage different customer types, built from patterns I observed across real accounts.

**Pilot Customers, Conversion Strategy.** Most CS people assume a pilot customer is already sold. If they were fully sold, they would sign a multi year agreement, not run a pilot. Pilots are evaluations. So the strategy cannot be passive. You create value before they fully commit, define a clear workflow so they actually use the platform, and position it as replacing a step in their process rather than adding one.

**Enterprise Customers, Adoption and Expansion.** Enterprise accounts have multi year agreements, complex needs, and multiple stakeholders. The big lesson from a large account: these customers get overwhelmed fast. We tried to configure too many things at once, and the internal champion coordinating input across managers hit a wall. The fix: focus on milestones, not volume. One item at a time. Set a cadence of weekly working sessions for 6 to 8 weeks upfront so momentum does not die between meetings.

**Complex Multi Year Customers, Game Plan.** For the most strategic accounts, often in safety critical industries, reliability is not optional. In high risk roles, screening for conscientiousness and dependability directly affects whether the right person ends up in the right seat. The playbook centers on milestone driven working sessions where each meeting moves one tangible thing forward: build one role, finalize one set of questions, review one set of results. Progress over perfection.

### 3. Cross Customer Insights

Where individual observations become organizational knowledge. Patterns I documented include:

**The stall then pivot pattern.** Multiple pilot accounts went through the same cycle: onboarded on a role, activity went quiet, and the role turned out to be on hold. Each time I reached out proactively and we pivoted to a different open position. This shaped the pilot playbook: do not wait for the customer, and always have a backup plan for when the first role falls through.

**The job title mismatch.** During a live onboarding session I was building a job blueprint with a customer and noticed the tasks did not match the position title they had given us. Every company names roles differently. We pivoted on the spot, picked a better blueprint, and the tasks aligned. This is why I align on traits and tasks first, not titles.

**The industry factor.** In some sectors, a broader talent shortage shapes adoption more than product quality does. Even a great screening tool cannot fix a shortage of qualified candidates in the pipeline. Naming that reality changed how I set expectations and where I focused effort.

### 4. Customer Intelligence Overview

The strategic layer that ties it together: how profiles feed playbooks, how playbooks inform engagement, and how cross customer insights drive continuous improvement. It also captures what we are actively doing in response to patterns, like creating early value through sample candidate evaluations when hiring is delayed, and positioning the platform as a replacement for phone screens rather than an extra step.

### 5. Customer Enablement Tools

The most common bottleneck to getting a customer live is gathering role specific input from busy hiring managers who do not always know how to articulate what they need. I built lightweight templates and outreach frameworks that make it easy for stakeholders to provide what is needed without overcomplicating the ask: traits and scenario templates, onboarding materials, integration guides, and results interpretation guides.

## How I Think About This Work

A consultant who works with our sales team called my role "the surgeon." Once an account is handed to me, I open it up. I look at the dashboard, the flow of candidates being invited, taking assessments, getting interviewed. I look for what is missing, what is stalling, where the gaps are. Then I act on what I find. I also step back. When I see a customer with candidates flowing in but nobody being marked as interviewed, that tells me something about how they are using the platform and what we are not measuring. The details and the big picture both matter.

## The Three Skills Behind This Work

**I recognize patterns.** The playbooks and insights came from paying attention across accounts. I noticed pilots stalling for the same reason. I noticed enterprise customers get overwhelmed when you configure too much at once. I turned observations into documented, repeatable strategy.

**I see gaps and fill them.** When I started there was no centralized view of customer health, no segmented engagement strategy, no shared knowledge base. The information lived in conversations and individual memory. The gap was not data, it was structure. I built the structure.

**When I see an opportunity, I act.** Nobody asked me to build this. I saw a team operating without the tools to scale and started building. I also cold called the account that became one of our most active customers. When something needs to happen, I do it, whether it is a system, a process, or a phone call.
